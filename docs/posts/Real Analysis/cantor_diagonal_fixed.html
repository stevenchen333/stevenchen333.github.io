Different sizes of infinity

1 Motivation

Can infinity itself be treated as a mathematical object, or is it merely a process that never ends?

For more than two thousand years, the concept of infinity has baffled philosophers, scientists, and mathematicians alike. At the heart of this fascination lies the seemingly simple question above.

For more than two thousand years, the concept of infinity has puzzled philosophers, scientists, and mathematicians alike. One of the earliest systematic discussions dates back to Ancient Greece, when Aristotle distinguished between potential infinity and actual infinity. The former describes an endless process: given any natural number \(n\), we can always construct a larger number \(n+1\). The latter treats infinity as a completed object, an idea Aristotle rejected.

In the seventeenth century, Galileo discovered something puzzling about infinity. Consider the natural numbers \(\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\) and the perfect squares \(\{1,4,9,\ldots\}.\)

At first glance, there seem to be fewer perfect squares, since not every natural number is a perfect square. However, we can match every natural number with exactly one perfect square: \(1\) with \(1\), \(2\) with \(4\), \(3\) with \(9\), and so on. No number in either collection is left unmatched! This became known as Galileo’s paradox: how can a smaller part of a collection have just as many numbers as the entire collection?

More than two centuries later, Georg Cantor developed a rigorous mathematical framework for comparing the sizes of infinite sets. His remarkable discovery was that not all infinities are equal. Some infinite sets are strictly larger than others.

One of the most elegant demonstrations of this discovery is Cantor’s diagonal argument.

In learning calculus, usually, the results we use are pretty intuitive. Derivative is instantaneous rate of change, and integral is the area under the curve. But we take for granted, often unknowingly, the assumptions needed for these results to hold. In beginning to study real analysis, we are taught to fortify our intuition and our way of thinking, by generalizing (relaxing assumptions), abstracting, questioning everything we learnt in calculus courses. We begin to realize, the assumptions we made in prior mathematical courses were very strong assumptions to be made. And as we begin to question ideas we once took for granted, we encounter mathematical results that challenge not only our intuition, but also our understanding of seemingly simple concepts. Cantor’s diagonal argument is the first proof that excites me because of how absurd, to a layman, the claim is. Then we are brought into the journey of the proof and realize how ingenious and beautiful the proof is.

We begin with some definitions. Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be two arbitrary sets.

Definition 1 (Injectivity, Surjectivity, Bijectivity (Schilling 2017)) A map \(f: X \to Y\) is called

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Injective (one-to-one)} \iff f(x) = f(x') \text{ implies } x = x', \\ \text{Surjective (onto)} \iff f(X) := \{f(x) \in Y : x \in X\} = Y, \\ \text{Bijective} \iff f \text{ is injective and surjective.} \end{aligned} \]

The cardinality of a set \(X\), denoted by \(\#X\), describes the number of elements in \(X\). For finite sets, this is simply the number of distinct elements. However, when dealing with infinite sets, the notion of counting becomes less straightforward. Instead, we compare the sizes of sets through one-to-one correspondences.

In particular, we say that two sets \(X\) and \(Y\) have the same cardinality, written as \[ \#X=\#Y, \] if there exists a bijection \(f:X\to Y\).

To get some more intuition about cardinality, we present one example by using the aforementioned Galileo’s paradox.

Example 1 Consider the set of natural numbers \[ \mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,4,\ldots\}, \] and the set of perfect squares \[ S=\{1,4,9,16,25,\ldots\}. \]

Clearly, \(S\subsetneq\mathbb{N}\), since every perfect square is a natural number, but not every natural number is a perfect square.

For finite sets, a proper subset must contain strictly fewer elements than the original set. It is therefore tempting to conclude that \(\#S<\#\mathbb{N}\).

However, consider the function \[ f:\mathbb{N}\to S,\qquad f(n)=n^2. \]

We can illustrate this correspondence as follows: \[ \begin{aligned} 1 &\longleftrightarrow 1,\\ 2 &\longleftrightarrow 4,\\ 3 &\longleftrightarrow 9,\\ 4 &\longleftrightarrow 16,\\ &\ \vdots \end{aligned} \]

Observe that this process is essentially a counting process, where we assign each element of the set of perfect squares a unique natural number. In other words, we can count the perfect squares one by one, without skipping or repeating any element. We talk about counting process after this example.

\(f\) is then a bijection:

  • Injective: If \(f(m)=f(n)\), then \(m^2=n^2\). Since \(m,n\in\mathbb{N}\), we must have \(m=n\).
  • Surjective: Every element of \(S\) is of the form \(n^2\) for some \(n\in\mathbb{N}\).

Thus, despite \(S\) being a proper subset of \(\mathbb{N}\), the two sets have the same cardinality: \[ \boxed{\#S=\#\mathbb{N}.} \]

The paradox arises because our finite intuition tells us that removing elements from a set must make it smaller. Yet, for infinite sets, this need not be the case.

Galileo’s paradox reveals something rather surprising: an infinite set can have the same cardinality as one of its proper subsets. In particular, even though the perfect squares form only a part of the natural numbers, we can still arrange them in a sequence where every perfect square is accounted for.

This observation motivates an important distinction. Suppose we are given an infinite set \(X\). Can we arrange all its elements in a list \[ x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots \]

such that every element of \(X\) appears somewhere in this list?

If such a listing is possible, we say that \(X\) is countably infinite. Informally, this means that its elements can be counted, one by one, using the natural numbers, even though the counting process never ends.

But what if no such listing is possible? What if, regardless of how we arrange the elements, there will always be some elements left out?

Such a set is called uncountable.

We formalize these notions in the following definition.

Definition 2 (Countable and Uncountable Sets) A set \(X\) is called countably infinite if there exists a bijection \[ f:\mathbb{N}\to X. \]

A set is called countable if it is either finite or countably infinite.

A set that is not countable is called uncountable.

We already encountered one example of a countably infinite set: the set of perfect squares. As demonstrated in Example 1, we can establish a bijection between \(\mathbb{N}\) and the perfect squares through \(f(n)=n^2\).

However, does this mean that every infinite set can be listed in this manner?

Remarkably, the answer is no. Cantor’s diagonal argument demonstrates that the real numbers in the interval \((0,1)\) cannot be enumerated, no matter how cleverly we attempt to arrange them.

Theorem 1 Cantor’s Diagonal Argument.

The set of real numbers in the interval \((0,1)\) is uncountable. That is, there exists no bijection between \(\mathbb{N}\) and \((0,1)\). \(\#(0,1)\) is called the continuum.

Proof. We proceed by contradiction.

Suppose that \((0,1)\) is countable. Then we can enumerate all its elements as \[ x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots \] where each \(x_i\in(0,1)\).

Writing each number in its decimal representation, we obtain \[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= 0.\mathbf{a_{11}}a_{12}a_{13}a_{14}\ldots\\ x_2 &= 0.a_{21}\mathbf{a_{22}}a_{23}a_{24}\ldots\\ x_3 &= 0.a_{31}a_{32}\mathbf{a_{33}}a_{34}\ldots\\ x_4 &= 0.a_{41}a_{42}a_{43}\mathbf{a_{44}}\ldots\\ &\vdots \end{aligned} \] where \(a_{ij}\in\{0,1,\ldots,9\}\) denotes the \(j\)-th decimal digit of \(x_i\). For numbers with two decimal representations, we choose the one that does not end in infinitely many 9s.

Now, we construct a new real number \[ y=0.b_1b_2b_3\ldots \] by defining \[ b_n= \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if }a_{nn}\neq 1,\\ 2, & \text{if }a_{nn}=1. \end{cases} \]

By construction, \(y\in(0,1)\), and its \(n\)-th decimal digit differs from the \(n\)-th decimal digit of \(x_n\).

In particular:

  • \(y\neq x_1\), since their first decimal digits differ.
  • \(y\neq x_2\), since their second decimal digits differ.
  • \(y\neq x_3\), since their third decimal digits differ.
  • And so on.

Consequently, \[ y\neq x_n,\qquad \forall n\in\mathbb{N}. \]

However, this contradicts our assumption that the sequence \(x_1,x_2,\ldots\) contains every real number in \((0,1)\), since \(y\in(0,1)\) but does not appear anywhere in the list.

Therefore, \((0,1)\) is uncountable. \(\square\)

TipChallenge: Are All Infinities Comparable?

Cantor’s diagonal argument tells us that

\[ \#\mathbb{N}<\#(0,1). \]

We now know that some infinite sets are larger than others. But can we always compare the sizes of two infinite sets?

Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are arbitrary infinite sets.

Is it necessarily true that

\[ \#X\leq\#Y \quad\text{or}\quad \#Y\leq\#X? \]

Could there exist two infinite sets for which neither inequality holds?

Does the answer depend on the axioms of set theory?
