Irrationality of Square Root of 2

Real Analysis
First proof I fell in love with

1 Motivation

​ This is the first theorem that hooks me into mathematics. If you are studying real analysis using Stephen Abbot’s Understanding analysis (Abbott 2015), this is the first and foremost thing the author discusses. You might or might not be as impressed as I was when I first looked at this beauty of a proof, but mathematicians all admit that this is a beautiful proof due to its simplicity.

​ Although there are a handful of ways to proof it, I will provide one here that I find attractive.

Theorem 1 (Irrationality of \(\sqrt{2}\)) There is no rational number whose square is 2 (Abbott 2015).

Proof. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that \(\sqrt{2}\) is rational. Then there exist integers \(p\) and \(q\), with \(q \neq 0\), such that

\[ \sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q}, \]

where \(p\) and \(q\) have no common factors other than 1. That is, the fraction \(\frac{p}{q}\) is in its lowest terms.

Squaring both sides, we obtain

\[ 2 = \frac{p^2}{q^2}, \]

which implies

\[ p^2 = 2q^2. \]

Thus, \(p^2\) is even. Since the square of an odd integer is odd, \(p\) must also be even.

Consequently, we can write \(p = 2k\) for some integer \(k\). Substituting this into the equation above gives

\[ (2k)^2 = 2q^2, \]

and hence

\[ q^2 = 2k^2. \]

Therefore, \(q^2\) is even, which implies that \(q\) is also even.

We have shown that both \(p\) and \(q\) are even. Hence, they are both divisible by 2, contradicting our assumption that \(p\) and \(q\) have no common factors other than 1.

Therefore, our original assumption must be false, and we conclude that

\[ \boxed{\sqrt{2} \notin \mathbb{Q}.} \]

TipChallenge: Can We Generalize This Result?

We have shown above that \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational. But is this property unique to the number 2? We know \(\sqrt{4} = 2\) so its rational. What about 3 or 5?

Challenge: Can we generalize this result to \(\sqrt{n}\) for any positive integer \(n\)? In other words, is the square root of every positive integer irrational?

If not, under what conditions is \(\sqrt{n}\) rational?

Hint: Consider the role of evenness in our proof. Can we replace it with a more general property of integers?
